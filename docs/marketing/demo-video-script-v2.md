# EDvanced Vue — Demo Script v2 (Restructured)

**What changed:** the *order*, not the voice. Roughly 80% of the client's original wording survives
word for word — it has been re-sequenced onto the problem→solution structure that the research
consensus says converts, with the three missing pieces (stakes, proof, CTA) added.

**Runtime:** 87.4s of narration in a 90-second master.
**Supersedes:** [demo-video-script.md](./demo-video-script.md) (picture) and
[demo-video-voiceover.md](./demo-video-voiceover.md) (VO) — both keyed to the original order.

---

## 1. What the research actually says

Sourced from seven current guides to SaaS demo scripting (links at the foot of this document). The
points below appeared in nearly all of them.

| Principle | The consensus |
|---|---|
| **Never open with the logo or the company name** | Cited as *the* most common mistake. "Never open with your company name, tagline, or logo animation. These mean nothing to someone discovering you for the first time." |
| **Problem first** | Open on the pain so the viewer thinks *"that's my problem"* before the product appears |
| **The first 3–8 seconds decide everything** | Steepest drop-off is between 5 and 15 seconds; 30% leave inside 30 seconds, 55% inside a minute |
| **Agitate — name the cost** | The problem must have a consequence attached, or there is no reason to keep watching |
| **Value proposition by ~0:30** | If the payoff arrives late, most of the audience never sees it |
| **Outcomes, not features** | "Narrate what changes for users, not just what buttons they click." Show one workflow well rather than listing many |
| **One proof point** | One concrete, quantified fact beats three vague ones |
| **One specific, low-friction CTA** | "Schedule a 15-minute demo" converts ~3× better than "Learn more" |
| **60–90 seconds** | The client's target is already correct |

The widely used spine is **Hook → Agitate → Value → Demo → Proof → CTA**, with a 60–90s allocation
of roughly: hook 0–8s, problem 8–25s, value prop 25–35s, demo 35–75s, proof 75–85s, CTA 85–90s.

## 2. Where the original script sits against that

The writing is genuinely good — the diagnosis below is about sequence and gaps, not quality. Five
findings, in order of how much they cost.

### 2.1 The best line in the script arrives at 1:05

> *"EDvanced Vue helps you monitor where you're headed — while there's still time to make decisions."*

That is the value proposition, the differentiator, and the most compelling sentence in the piece.
It currently sits at **70% through the video** — past the point where, per the retention data, more
than half the audience has already left. The client's own note on it reads *"this is the key
message."* It is. **It belongs in the first fifteen seconds, not the last twenty-five.**

This single move is the most valuable change in this document.

### 2.2 The video opens on the logo and a greeting

> *"Welcome to EDvanced Vue."*

This is the single most-cited anti-pattern in the research. The first eight seconds are the most
expensive real estate in the video, and a greeting plus a logo spends them on information that means
nothing to a superintendent who has never heard the name. The name is better *earned* around 0:22 —
after the viewer has recognised their own problem and wants to know what fixes it.

### 2.3 The problem is named but never costed

> *"School finance teams have plenty of reports... and plenty of data. But the challenge is being able to quickly see what that data is telling you."*

True, and well phrased — but nothing bad happens in it. There is no consequence, so there is nothing
to feel. The research calls this the agitate step, and it is the difference between *"yes, that's
accurate"* and *"yes, and it cost us."* One added sentence fixes it, and the original script already
contains the raw material: *waiting until year-end to see where you landed*.

### 2.4 Three of the strongest seconds are spent on origin before the problem lands

> *"EDvanced Vue was created by a school finance professional…"*

For this market that founder credential is a real asset — arguably the most persuasive fact in the
whole video for a business official. But at 29 words landing at 0:15, it occupies the
highest-drop-off window in the video with credibility for a product the viewer has not yet decided
they need. Compressed to a single clause and moved behind the value proposition, it does more work
in half the time.

### 2.5 There is no proof point and no call to action

The script ends on the tagline. Nothing asks the viewer to do anything, and no fact is offered to
support the claims. Every source treats a single specific CTA as non-negotiable — and it is the
cheapest possible addition, since it costs about six seconds.

## 3. The restructured script

Client wording is preserved wherever possible. `[NEW]` marks added lines; everything unmarked is
their language, sometimes with a word or two of connective tissue.

### HOOK — 0:00–0:08 · *problem-first, no logo*

> **VO:** "Most districts find out where they landed… ▌ after the year is already over." `[NEW — built from their word "landed"]`

**Visual:** A fiscal-year timeline runs left to right and stops hard at a year-end marker. Behind it,
a figure resolves that nobody wanted to see. Cold, still, no branding anywhere on screen.

> Built from the client's own phrase — *"waiting until year-end to see where you landed"* — so the
> hook is in their voice, not a new one. No name, no logo, no welcome.

> **VO:** "By then, the decisions that could have changed it are behind you." `[NEW — the missing stakes]`

> **VO:** "School finance teams have plenty of reports… and plenty of data. But seeing what that data is telling you — in time to act on it — is a different problem." `[client's, with four words added]`

**Visual:** Reports and spreadsheets fan into frame, overlapping, misaligned, unreadable. The
calendar keeps advancing behind them.

> The only edit to their sentence is *"in time to act on it"*, which converts an accurate

### VALUE PROPOSITION — 0:22–0:31 · *the key message, moved to where it counts*

> **VO:** "EDvanced Vue helps you monitor where you're headed. ▌ Not just where you've been." `[client's key line, relocated]`

**Visual:** The paper organises itself into the Executive Dashboard, building empty. First appearance

> **VO:** "Built by a school finance professional, it turns the data you already have into information you can use." `[client's, 29 words → 18]`

**Visual:** Dashboard finishes assembling; figures populate and count up.

### DEMO 1 · Executive Dashboard — 0:39–0:52

> **VO:** "Log in, and your district's financial health is on one screen. ▌ Projected fund balance. ▌ Days of operating cash. ▌ Revenues and expenditures. ▌ And, most importantly, the areas that need your attention."

**Visual:** Each named tile lifts and brightens while the others sit back at 45%. On the last line the

> Preserved almost exactly. The four-part list is the one place where feature-naming is correct —
> these are the four numbers the audience already cares about, and naming them is recognition rather
> than feature-listing.

### DEMO 2 · Revenue Dashboard — 0:52–1:04

> **VO:** "When something looks off, you follow it — ▌ where your revenues are coming from, compare budget to actuals, which variances are significant, and how the year is trending." `[opener changed; the four clauses are theirs]`

**Visual:** One continuous shot of `/revenues`; four cards highlight in turn as each clause is named.

> *"From there, you can take a deeper look"* became *"When something looks off, you follow it"* —
> same function, but motivated by the alert we just saw rather than by the tour. Outcome, not
> navigation.

> **VO:** "The same for expenditures, fund balance, and cash position. ▌ And a forecast that shows where the year ends — while there's still time to change it." `[client's list + their "still time" phrase]`

**Visual:** Three fast beats — expenditures, fund balance, cash — then the forecast projection line
draws *forward* past today and crosses the dashed policy target, which flashes amber.

> The forecast is the product's actual argument, so it closes the demo rather than sitting mid-list.
> The hook's dead-ended timeline is now answered by a line that keeps drawing.

> **VO:** "EDvanced Vue. ▌ See the story behind the numbers." `[client's, untouched]`

## 5. What the client still has to decide

### 5.1 The proof line (six seconds, currently blank)

One fact, quantified, in their own voice. In rough order of persuasive power for this audience:

1. **Adoption** — *"Districts across [state] use EDvanced Vue to walk into board meetings knowing exactly what the numbers say."* Strongest if there are enough districts to name a number.
2. **An outcome** — a district that caught a shortfall early and what that was worth. Most persuasive of all, and the hardest to get cleared for use.
3. **The credential, doing double duty** — if adoption numbers are still thin, the founder line moves here and becomes the proof: *"Built by a school finance professional who spent [N] years closing these books."* This costs nothing and is available immediately.

If none is available at recording time, **cut the section** and let the video run at 81 seconds. An
empty proof beat is worse than no proof beat.

### 5.2 The CTA offer

"Book a fifteen-minute walkthrough" is the placeholder because specific, time-bounded CTAs measurably
outperform generic ones. If the real motion is a form, a phone call, or a conference booth, say so
and the line changes — but it should name a specific, small, low-risk next step.

### 5.3 Two lines that were cut, and what it would cost to restore them

| Cut line | Why | To restore |
|---|---|---|
| *"Welcome to EDvanced Vue."* | Opens on the name — the most-cited anti-pattern | Only in the "gentle" variant, §6 |
| *"Because the numbers are only part of the story."* | Lovely, but it restates the tagline nine words before the tagline arrives | Costs 4s — trim the proof beat to compensate |
| Closing tri-colon (*"see what's happening, understand what needs your attention, and tell the financial story…"*) | 21 words that restate the demo; the CTA needs the time more | Costs 8s — would require dropping proof entirely |

## 6. If the client wants to keep their opening

If *"Welcome to EDvanced Vue"* is non-negotiable — and it may be, since some clients want the name
first for brand reasons — this is the smallest change set that captures most of the benefit:

1. **Keep** the original opening and its order for the first 15 seconds.
2. **Move** the key message ("monitor where you're headed / while there's still time") from 1:05 to
   immediately after the origin line, around 0:27. *This is the change worth fighting for.* Everything
   else on this list is optional; this one is not.
3. **Add** the cost sentence — *"By then, the decisions that could have changed it are behind you."*
4. **Add** the proof line and the CTA at the end.
5. **Cut** the closing tri-colon to make room.

That keeps the script recognisably theirs while fixing the two gaps that actually cost conversions —
a buried value proposition and a missing CTA. My recommendation is still the full restructure in §3,
but this variant is a real improvement over the original and an easier internal sell.
